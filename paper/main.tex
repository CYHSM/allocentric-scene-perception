\begin{abstract}
\TODO{Written last.} Two images of a place can differ for two reasons: the place
changed, or the observer moved. We render a dataset in which both are varied
independently and measure them in the same currency---distance in a frozen
encoder's embedding---which lets them be traded against one another. The
resulting quantity, $\lambda(\Delta)$, is the distance landmarks must physically
move to cost a model as much as walking $\Delta$ degrees around the scene, and it
is reported in metres.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\TODO{$\sim$0.75 page.} The Four Mountains Test asks a person to recognise a
place from a new viewpoint, and dissociates allocentric from egocentric spatial
memory. Image benchmarks cannot make that dissociation, because ``the same
place'' and ``the same picture'' are confounded whenever the viewpoint is held
fixed. Our contribution is a dataset in which the two are orthogonal by
construction, and a read-out with units.

\section{The benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\paragraph{One set of scenes, rendered five ways.}
Each scene is sampled once---landmark positions, uniform sizes, one camera
rig---and rendered in five stimulus modes (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset}, top).
Scene \texttt{a007} in \texttt{c0} and in \texttt{c4} is the same place from the
same spot; only the cues that distinguish landmarks, and the surrounding world,
differ. Mode is therefore a within-scene factor and every cross-mode comparison
is paired. \TODO{cite/describe the five modes.}

\paragraph{Scene distance.}
A scene is a set of (identity, position) pairs. Two scenes differ along three
coordinates, each computed under an optimal assignment between landmark sets:
$\dpos$, the largest distance a landmark must move, in metres; $\dbind$, the
fraction of places whose occupant changed; and $\did$, how different the sets of
landmarks are. The three are orthogonal by construction---a permutation gives
$\dpos = 0$ exactly---which a slot-matched displacement measure does not
achieve. We sweep $\dpos$, because it is the coordinate with units; identity is
varied by the mode ladder instead.

\paragraph{Anchors and probes.}
\TODO{$N$} mutually distinct anchor scenes form the retrieval gallery.
\TODO{$K$} of them additionally carry a probe ladder: the same scene with one
landmark displaced $1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24$ m (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset}, bottom).
Every scene---anchor or probe---is rendered identically under two appearances at
eight bearings, so nothing is a distractor at render time and all analyses are
post hoc.

\paragraph{$\lambda$: trading metres against degrees.}
Let $D_{\text{view}}(\Delta)$ be the embedding distance between an anchor and
itself seen $\Delta$ degrees away, and $D_{\text{scene}}(d)$ the distance between
an anchor and its probe at $\dpos = d$, at the same bearing. Both use the same
appearance contrast. Then
\begin{equation}
  \lambda(\Delta) = \{\, d : D_{\text{scene}}(d) = D_{\text{view}}(\Delta) \,\},
  \label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
the distance the landmarks must move to cost the model as much as the turn.
Small $\lambda$ is the good end: a representation that survives the turn pays
little for it, so a small displacement suffices to match the cost. A
$\lambda$ of tens of metres indicates appearance matching. Where the crossing
falls outside the probe ladder we report $\lambda$ as unmeasured rather than
clamping it to an endpoint.
