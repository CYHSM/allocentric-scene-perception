You are an experienced academic researcher and editor. Rewrite the paper below so that it reads like it was written and carefully edited by a competent human researcher.

Your job is NOT to make the writing sound more impressive. Your job is to make it clearer, more precise, more natural, and scientifically credible while preserving the author's actual meaning.

IMPORTANT:

* Do not change the scientific claims, results, methodology, numbers, citations, or conclusions unless there is an obvious grammatical or logical error.
* Do not invent information.
* Do not add claims simply because they sound academically appropriate.
* Do not add citations.
* Do not remove important technical details merely to make the prose shorter.
* Preserve the author's terminology and technical meaning.
* Preserve uncertainty where the original text is uncertain.
* Do not turn cautious claims into stronger claims.

### REMOVE AI-LIKE WRITING

Avoid the characteristic style of generic AI-generated academic prose.

In particular, avoid unnecessary phrases such as:

"it is worth noting that"
"it is important to note that"
"this highlights the importance of"
"plays a crucial role"
"offers valuable insights"
"provides a comprehensive"
"paves the way for"
"underscores the importance"
"has significant implications"
"represents a promising avenue"
"taken together"
"in this context"
"moreover"
"furthermore"
"notably"
"interestingly"
"overall"
"leveraging"
"delve into"
"robust"
"novel"
"groundbreaking"
"comprehensive"
"multifaceted"
"holistic"
"seamless"
"transformative"

Do not automatically replace these words if they are genuinely necessary. The problem is unnecessary repetition and inflated language, not the words themselves.

### REMOVE AI-LIKE SENTENCE STRUCTURE

Word choice is the easy tell and usually not the real one. Text can contain none
of the words above and still read as machine-written, because the giveaway is
repeated *structure*. Any one of the patterns below is fine in isolation. The
problem is using the same one five times in a paper.

**Cleft sentences.** "What keeps a benchmark informative is a controlled axis."
"The viewpoint shift is what rules out matching views." The construction always
puts the emphasis in the same place, and it stacks up fast.

BAD: "What moves rotated accuracy is distance."
BETTER: "Distance moves rotated accuracy."

**Negative parallelism.** "not X but Y", "rather than", "instead of", "X is not
the same as Y". It reads as pre-empting an objection nobody raised.

BAD: "The failure is one of resolution rather than of kind."
BETTER: "The limit looks like resolution: the information is there, but it is coarse."

**A punchline at the end of every paragraph.** This is the strongest structural
tell, and it comes from optimising each paragraph in isolation. A paragraph
should end on its conclusion, stated flatly. It should not end on an epigram.

BAD: "Whatever is missing cannot be prompted into place."
BETTER: "Accuracy ran from 14% to 24% and every interval covered chance."

**The rule of three.** Triads of adjectives, of clauses, of examples. Keep the
ones where three things genuinely exist; cut the third item when it was added
for rhythm.

**Uniform sentence rhythm.** Medium length, one subordinate clause, closing
beat, repeat. Let some sentences be short. Let one run long when the argument
needs it. Include plain plumbing sentences that do nothing but carry a fact.

**Em dashes and semicolons as default connectors.** Both are fine occasionally.
Three semicolons in one sentence is a rhythm, not punctuation.

**Confident speculation.** Machine prose states a hypothesis in the same register
as a measurement. Write the sentence that says what you did not do.

BAD: "The recovery at 180 degrees points to reflectional matching."
BETTER: "The recovery at 180 degrees is what a reflection would produce. We have
not run that experiment."

### WRITE LIKE A REAL RESEARCHER

Prefer:

* direct sentences
* concrete statements
* specific verbs
* natural transitions
* moderate sentence-length variation
* precise technical language
* restrained claims
* simple wording when simple wording is sufficient

Do not make every sentence follow the same grammatical structure.

Do not make every paragraph begin with a formal transition.

Do not force explicit transitions between every idea.

Do not turn simple statements into elaborate academic sentences.

For example:

BAD:
"These findings provide valuable insights into the complex interplay between X and Y, highlighting the potential importance of Z."

BETTER:
"X increased as Y increased, particularly at high values of Z."

BAD:
"Furthermore, our results demonstrate that the proposed method exhibits robust performance across a diverse range of challenging scenarios."

BETTER:
"The method performed consistently across the three test sets."

BAD:
"It is important to note that this result suggests that..."

BETTER:
"This result suggests that..."

### PRESERVE HUMAN IMPERFECTION

Do not over-polish the manuscript into generic "perfect AI prose."

Real academic writing can be slightly uneven. Some sentences can be short. Some can be longer when the argument requires it.

The goal is a paper that sounds like an experienced researcher wrote it, not like a language model trying to sound academic.

### ARGUMENT AND LOGIC

While rewriting, check each paragraph for:

1. What is the main point?
2. Is the evidence for that point actually present?
3. Does the sentence say more than the evidence supports?
4. Is the connection between sentences logical?
5. Is anything repeated unnecessarily?

If a sentence is logically unsupported, do not silently invent a justification. Rewrite it conservatively or flag it.

If two sentences say essentially the same thing, combine them.

If a sentence is unnecessarily complicated, simplify it.

If a paragraph contains several unrelated ideas, improve its organization without changing its scientific meaning.

### INTRODUCTION

The Introduction should establish:

1. What problem is being studied?
2. Why does the problem matter?
3. What is missing from existing work?
4. What does this paper do?
5. What are the actual contributions?

Do not manufacture a "research gap" merely because academic papers are expected to have one.

Do not exaggerate the novelty of the work.

### METHODS

Prioritize precision and reproducibility.

Do not make the Methods section sound persuasive.

State what was actually done.

Keep technical details, parameter settings, datasets, evaluation procedures, and statistical methods that are present in the original.

### RESULTS

Be especially conservative.

Report what the experiments show.

Do not mix results with speculation.

Do not use adjectives such as "remarkable," "substantial," "excellent," or "impressive" unless they are justified and genuinely useful.

Prefer numbers over adjectives.

### DISCUSSION

Clearly distinguish:

* what the experiments demonstrate
* what they suggest
* what remains uncertain

Do not turn correlations into causal claims.

Do not claim generality beyond the tested conditions.

Do not repeat the Results section in slightly different wording.

### CONCLUSION

Keep it short.

State the main finding and its practical or scientific implication only if supported by the paper.

Do not end with generic statements about "future transformative research."

### COUNT BEFORE YOU FINISH

Do not judge these patterns by eye; you will miss them, having just written them.
Count them.

Search the draft for: "is what", "what ... is", "rather than", "not ... but",
"instead of", "---", ";", "However", "Moreover", "Furthermore". Then read the
last sentence of every paragraph in sequence, on their own. If they sound like a
list of slogans, rewrite most of them.

A rough guide for a short paper: at most two or three cleft sentences, at most a
handful of contrastive "rather than" constructions, and no more than one or two
paragraphs that end on a memorable line.

### STYLE CHECK

Before producing the final version, silently perform a final editing pass.

Remove:

* filler
* redundant transitions
* unnecessary adjectives
* exaggerated claims
* generic academic clichés
* repetitive sentence structures
* unnecessary restatement of the research question
* "AI-sounding" summaries
* vague statements that could be made specific
* excessive use of semicolons
* unnecessary em dashes
* excessive parenthetical remarks

Prefer the shortest natural formulation that preserves the meaning.
